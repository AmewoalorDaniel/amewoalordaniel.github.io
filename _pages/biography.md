### Background

I started my undergraduate degree in Biochemistry at KNUST with a simple curiosity: how does the human body work, and what goes wrong in disease? What drew me in was not medicine itself, but the question underneath:*why* do cells malfunction, *how* do pathogens exploit molecular machinery, and *what* can we learn from the biology of disease to build better interventions? That curiosity led me to join a research lab before my second year ended.

My first research experience came through an internship at the **Borquaye Research Group**, where I studied the antimicrobial and antioxidant properties of medicinal plant extracts. Working alongside experienced researchers, I learned how to culture bacteria, perform susceptibility assays, and use cheminformatics tools to model protein-ligand interactions. That work taught me the rigor of experimental design and what it means to pursue a scientific question systematically.

### Developing a Research Identity

The formative experience of my undergraduate years was my dissertation project, supervised by **Dr. Alexander Kwarteng**, where I designed a multi-epitope subunit vaccine against lymphatic filariasis, a parasitic neglected tropical disease. This was my first fully computational project, and it introduced me to immunoinformatics, epitope prediction, molecular docking, and in silico immune simulation. I was excited by a simple idea: a researcher with a laptop and the right tools could design a vaccine candidate, characterize its molecular interactions with immune receptors, and predict the immune responses, before setting foot in a wet laboratory. The project was recognised at multiple conferences, including the Molecular Parasitology Meeting at Woods Hole, USA.

Around the same period, I joined the **Human Genetics and Genomics Lab (HuGene Lab)** under **Dr. Lord J.J. Gowans**, contributing to a genome-wide association study investigating Alzheimer's disease susceptibility loci in Ghanaians. This was my first immersion in population genetics. This experience introduced me to population genetics and strengthened my appreciation for the experimental foundations of genomics. Through DNA extraction, quantification, PCR amplification, and gel electrophoresis, I gained hands-on experience that complemented my growing computational interests and strengthened my curiosity about how genetic variation shapes disease risk.

### Current Work

After graduating with First Class Honours, I transitioned to full-time research. As a **Teaching and Research Assistant** in Dr. Gowans' laboratory, I contributed to the genetic characterization of rare craniofacial and skeletal disorders, performing variant annotation and prioritization across patients with craniosynostosis and mandibuloacral dysplasia. One project involved identifying a recurrent pathogenic *LMNA* variant in a Ghanaian multiplex family, which showed me how genomic analysis could yield clinically actionable findings even in under-resourced settings. It reinforced my belief that genomics has an essential role to play in the future of African healthcare.

Alongside this work, I pursued independent computational projects exploring cancer biology and immunogenomics. One focused on ferroptosis regulatory programs in tumour-associated macrophages across multiple cancers, while another investigated whether alterations in the oxeiptosis pathway could predict immunotherapy response and neoantigen vaccine candidacy in TCGA cohorts. Working on these projects pushed me to learn and apply existing multi-omics analysis pipelines, and sharpened my ability to think about biological problems from a systems-level perspective.

My current MPhil research brings many of these interests together. Using **whole exome sequencing**, I investigate whether individuals with syndromic orofacial clefts carry rare germline variants in genes associated with hereditary cancer susceptibility. This question has real implications for cancer risk surveillance in affected Ghanaian families, and sits at the intersection of rare disease genetics, cancer genomics, and African population health.

### Looking Forward

I hope to build a research career at the intersection of cancer genomics, tumour immunology, and human genetics, integrating computational and experimental approaches to understand how molecular alterations drive disease and how those discoveries can be translated into better diagnostics and therapies. I am especially motivated by the potential of genomics to improve health outcomes in African populations, whose genetic diversity and disease burden remain significantly underrepresented in global biomedical research.
